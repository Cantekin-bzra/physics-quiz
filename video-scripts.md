## Question 1 — Motion and v-t diagrams

> A tram travels along a straight line. Its velocity is −8.0 m/s and its acceleration is −2.0 m/s². What happens to the speed of the tram?

**Correct answer A:** The speed increases: the tram gets faster in the negative direction.

### `q1b` — Negative acceleration means braking

*Wrong option B: The speed decreases, because a negative acceleration always means braking.*  
84 words, about 34 seconds

A minus sign in front of an acceleration does not mean slowing down. It only tells you the direction: the acceleration points in the negative direction. Whether an object speeds up or slows down depends on how velocity and acceleration compare. If they have the same sign, the object speeds up. If they have opposite signs, it slows down. Here both are negative, so they point the same way and the tram gets faster. Braking would need a positive acceleration against this negative velocity.

### `q1c` — Signs cancel out

*Wrong option C: The speed stays constant, because the two negative signs cancel each other out.*  
79 words, about 32 seconds

Signs only cancel when you multiply or divide, not when you compare two different quantities. Velocity and acceleration are separate physical quantities measured in different units: metres per second and metres per second squared. You cannot cancel one against the other. A constant speed would require an acceleration of exactly zero. Here the acceleration is two metres per second squared, so after one second the velocity has changed from minus eight to minus ten. The speed has clearly changed.

### `q1d` — Expecting a turning point

*Wrong option D: The speed decreases to zero and then the tram moves in the opposite direction.*  
77 words, about 31 seconds

A turning point happens only when the velocity passes through zero, and that needs an acceleration pointing against the motion. Think of a ball thrown upwards: the velocity is positive, the acceleration is negative, so the ball slows, stops and comes back. Our tram is different. Its velocity is already negative and the acceleration pushes it further in that same negative direction. The velocity moves away from zero, not towards it, so the tram never turns around.

## Question 2 — Free fall and vertical throws

> A ball is thrown vertically upwards. At the highest point of its flight, what are its velocity and its acceleration?

**Correct answer A:** v = 0 m/s and a = 9.81 m/s², directed downwards.

### `q2b` — Zero velocity means zero acceleration

*Wrong option B: v = 0 m/s and a = 0 m/s², because the ball is not moving at that instant.*  
80 words, about 32 seconds

Velocity and acceleration are independent. Velocity tells you how fast the position changes. Acceleration tells you how fast the velocity changes. At the highest point the velocity is zero for one instant, but it is still changing: a moment earlier it was upwards, a moment later it is downwards. That change is the acceleration, and it is nine point eight one metres per second squared. If the acceleration really were zero, the ball would stop in mid-air and stay there.

### `q2c` — Motion needs a force in its own direction

*Wrong option C: v = 0 m/s and a points upwards, otherwise the ball could not have risen at all.*  
79 words, about 32 seconds

This is the old idea that a moving object carries a force with it. It does not. The hand gave the ball a velocity, not a supply of upward force. Once the ball has left the hand, the only force acting on it is its weight, and the weight always points downwards. The ball rises because it already has upward velocity, while gravity is busy reducing that velocity. No upward force is needed at any point of the flight.

### `q2d` — Acceleration depends on speed

*Wrong option D: v = 0 m/s and a is smaller than 9.81 m/s², because the ball has almost stopped.*  
83 words, about 33 seconds

The acceleration in free fall does not depend on how fast the object is moving. It comes from Newton's second law: the acceleration equals the resultant force divided by the mass. The only force here is the weight, which is mass times g, so the mass cancels and the acceleration is g at every single point of the flight. Going up, at the top, coming down: always nine point eight one metres per second squared downwards. Speed simply does not enter the calculation.

## Question 3 — Statics and contact forces

> A block of mass 5.0 kg lies at rest on a ramp inclined at 30° to the horizontal. How large is the normal force on the block?

**Correct answer A:** 42.5 N

### `q3b` — The normal force always equals the weight

*Wrong option B: 49.1 N*  
87 words, about 35 seconds

On a horizontal table the normal force does equal the weight, so it is tempting to make that a rule. It is not. The normal force is always perpendicular to the surface, and on a ramp that direction is tilted. Only the component of the weight pressing into the ramp has to be balanced. The other component pulls the block down the slope and is held by friction instead. So the normal force is the weight times the cosine of the angle: forty two point five newtons.

### `q3c` — Sine and cosine swapped

*Wrong option C: 24.5 N*  
90 words, about 36 seconds

Twenty four point five newtons is the weight times the sine of thirty degrees. That is the correct size of a real force, but it is the wrong one: it is the component along the slope, the part that tries to make the block slide. The perpendicular component uses the cosine. A quick check: as the ramp gets flatter, the angle goes to zero, and the normal force must approach the full weight. Cosine of zero is one, sine of zero is zero, so cosine is the one you need.

### `q3d` — Dividing instead of multiplying

*Wrong option D: 56.6 N*  
82 words, about 33 seconds

Fifty six point six newtons is the weight divided by the cosine of thirty. Dividing has made the normal force larger than the weight itself, and that should immediately look wrong: the block cannot press into the ramp harder than gravity pulls it down. When you split a vector into components, each component is always smaller than or equal to the original vector, so you multiply by cosine or sine, you never divide. Use the size of your answer as a check.

## Question 4 — Dynamics and Newton's second law

> A lorry drives at a constant speed of 60 km/h along a straight, flat road. The engine provides a driving force of 2400 N. What is the resultant force on the lorry?

**Correct answer A:** 0 N — the driving force and the resistance forces cancel exactly.

### `q4b` — Motion requires a resultant force

*Wrong option B: 2400 N in the direction of travel — without a resultant force the lorry would stop.*  
83 words, about 33 seconds

This is the most common wrong idea in all of mechanics: that movement needs a force to keep it going. Newton's first law says the opposite. An object keeps its velocity unless a resultant force changes it. Force does not maintain motion, it changes motion. The lorry needs its engine only because friction and air resistance are pulling backwards. Cancel those and you need no engine force at all. The driving force is not the resultant force, it is one force among several.

### `q4c` — Believing you always need the mass

*Wrong option C: It cannot be calculated, because the mass of the lorry is not given.*  
78 words, about 31 seconds

Newton's second law says the resultant force is mass times acceleration. Constant speed on a straight road means the velocity is not changing, so the acceleration is exactly zero. Anything multiplied by zero is zero, and that includes an unknown mass. The resultant force is zero newtons whether the lorry weighs three tonnes or thirty. Missing information is only a problem if the answer actually depends on it. Check the acceleration first, and sometimes the unknown quantity disappears.

### `q4d` — Resistance must beat the drive

*Wrong option D: 2400 N backwards — the resistance forces must be larger to hold the speed steady.*  
82 words, about 33 seconds

If the backwards forces really were larger than the driving force, the resultant force would point backwards and the lorry would slow down. That contradicts the constant speed we were given. Equilibrium does not mean one side wins, it means both sides are exactly equal. The resistance forces add up to two thousand four hundred newtons, the same as the driving force, and the two cancel. The lorry then keeps whatever velocity it already had, which is precisely what the question describes.

## Question 5 — Uniform circular motion

> A ball is swung on a string in a horizontal circle at constant speed. The string suddenly breaks. Seen from above, which path does the ball follow immediately afterwards?

**Correct answer A:** It flies off in a straight line, along the tangent to the circle.

### `q5b` — The centrifugal force

*Wrong option B: It flies straight outwards, away from the centre, pushed by the centrifugal force.*  
86 words, about 34 seconds

There is no outward force on the ball. The only horizontal force was the string pulling inwards, and that is the centripetal force. What you feel as an outward push when you swing something is your hand resisting the inward pull. The ball never had an outward force, so when the string breaks there is nothing to send it outwards. Its velocity at that moment points along the tangent, and that is the direction it takes. Watch a hammer thrower: the hammer leaves sideways, not radially.

### `q5c` — Curved motion carries on by itself

*Wrong option C: It keeps curving for a short time and then gradually straightens out.*  
86 words, about 34 seconds

An object does not store the shape of its path. Curving requires a resultant force pointing towards the centre at every instant, supplied here by the string. The moment the string breaks that force is gone, and it is gone completely, not gradually. From that instant on the resultant horizontal force is zero and the motion is a straight line. There is no fading memory of the circle. The change from curve to straight line is immediate, not something that happens over the next half second.

### `q5d` — The spiral path

*Wrong option D: It moves outwards along a spiral path.*  
82 words, about 33 seconds

A spiral would mean the ball is still turning while also moving away from the centre, and turning always needs a force towards the centre. After the string breaks there is no such force, so no turning can happen. The distance from the centre does grow, which is probably what makes a spiral feel right, but it grows along a straight line, not a curve. Draw the tangent and follow it: the ball gets further from the centre while travelling perfectly straight.

## Question 6 — Work, energy and power

> A student carries a box of mass 12 kg at constant speed along a horizontal corridor, a distance of 15 m. How much work does the carrying force do on the box?

**Correct answer A:** 0 J

### `q6b` — Treating distance as height

*Wrong option B: 1766 J*  
84 words, about 34 seconds

One thousand seven hundred and sixty six joules is mass times g times fifteen metres. That is the formula for lifting work, and the fifteen metres in it must be a height. Here the fifteen metres is measured horizontally, so the box is never lifted. Its potential energy depends only on height, and the height does not change by a single centimetre. Lifting work applies when a body goes up or down. When it travels sideways, this formula simply does not fit the situation.

### `q6c` — Forgetting g in the weight

*Wrong option C: 180 J*  
81 words, about 32 seconds

One hundred and eighty joules is twelve times fifteen: mass times distance. Mass is not a force, so this product is not work at all. Check the units. Work is measured in joules, and a joule is a newton times a metre. Kilograms times metres gives you neither. To turn a mass into a force you must multiply by g. Doing a quick unit check on every answer catches this kind of error before it costs you marks in the exam.

### `q6d` — Confusing work with power

*Wrong option D: It depends on how fast the student walks.*  
78 words, about 31 seconds

Time matters for power, not for work. Work is force times displacement, and neither of those contains time. Power is work divided by time, so walking faster does change the power. But the work stays the same: carrying the box quickly or slowly along the same corridor does exactly the same amount of work on it, which here is zero. Whenever a question mentions speed or duration, check whether it is really asking about work or about power.
